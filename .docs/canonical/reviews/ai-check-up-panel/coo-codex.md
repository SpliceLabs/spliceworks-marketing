## Blind buyer read

I’d answer this as a typical 150-person services firm: useful individual use,
a few pilots, no owned operating model, no agent-run production workflow. I
would hesitate on several questions because I would need to ask IT, delivery
leaders or finance rather than know the answer myself. That is important: this
should assess the company, not reward executive confidence.

## Questions

1–4. **How far does AI go in [area]?**  
**My answer:** core work 2; customers 2; back office 1; management 2. I
hesitated between 1 and 2 because private ChatGPT use can genuinely help work
without being part of an agreed process.  
**Review:** The progression is good, but rung 1 overlaps rung 2, and “agents”
is jargon until defined. Rungs 3–5 are a sensible maturity ladder. **TWEAK.**
Replace the shared options with:

- **[1] It is not part of this work**  
  People may use a chat tool privately, but it is not part of the workflow.
- **[2] It helps people with the work**  
  People use AI to draft, summarise or research, but the workflow is unchanged.
- **[3] It completes repeatable steps**  
  AI completes defined steps in the workflow; people still start and finish it.
- **[4] It runs the workflow with people deciding key points**  
  AI starts and carries out the workflow; people make the decisions that matter.
- **[5] It runs and improves the workflow**  
  AI runs the workflow end to end and improves it from results; people set goals.

5. **How many people use AI in real work every week?**  
**My answer:** 20 to 40%. I would be guessing. “Beyond the odd chat” is clear,
but the percentage bands imply evidence I probably do not have.  
**Review:** Sensible order and scores. It invites flattering estimates. **TWEAK.**
Question: “Roughly what share of your people use AI in their real work most
weeks?” Use “Fewer than 1 in 5”, “About 2 to 4 in 10”, and so on.

6. **Who owns AI?**  
**My answer:** informal champions. No hesitation.  
**Review:** Clear, but jumping from 2 to 4 hides a common state: a named,
part-time owner without budget. **REWRITE.**

- [1] Nobody yet
- [2] Informal champions
- [3] A named person, alongside their main job
- [4] A named person with time and responsibility for it
- [5] A leader or team, with a budget

7. **What does AI know about your business?**  
**My answer:** whatever people paste in. I would hesitate because different
tools know different things.  
**Review:** “It” is too broad, and the top option is implausibly absolute.
Good scoring intent, weak operational wording. **REWRITE.**

Question: “For the AI used in important work, how much approved company context
can it use?”

- [1] Only what people paste in
- [3] Selected documents or knowledge we have connected
- [5] Current documents and approved system data, with experts maintaining it

8. **How does AI reach your systems and data?**  
**My answer:** people copy and paste. I would need IT to confirm the rest.  
**Review:** This is the weakest question for a non-technical COO. Two answers
score 1, while “broad keys”, “scoped access” and “audit trail” are jargon.
The maturity order is right. **REWRITE.**

- [1] People move information in and out manually
- [2] Tools have shared logins or more access than they need
- [4] IT-approved connections link tools to specific systems
- [5] Each tool gets only the access it needs; permissions and activity are recorded

9. **How is AI’s work checked before it counts?**  
**My answer:** each person decides. No hesitation.  
**Review:** Clear enough, but “reviews everything” versus automatic checks
misses the common risk-based review model. **TWEAK.** Replace the last two:

- [3] A person checks every item, or checks higher-risk work
- [5] Automatic checks catch routine errors; people approve important decisions

10. **Do you measure what AI changes?**  
**My answer:** not measured. I might call anecdotal feedback “rough estimates”
if trying to sound better than reality.  
**Review:** Right subject, but “baseline” is jargon and rough estimates score
surprisingly well. **REWRITE.**

Question: “For important AI work, how do you know whether it has improved
anything?”

- [1] We do not measure it
- [2] We track use, but not the outcome
- [3] We rely on rough estimates or feedback
- [4] We measure outcomes, but have no before-and-after comparison
- [5] We measure each workflow against a before figure

11. **What rules cover AI use?**  
**My answer:** a draft policy, which does not fit cleanly.  
**Review:** A written policy can be dormant, and the top answer bundles four
different controls. It will encourage generous interpretation. **REWRITE.**

- [1] None yet
- [2] Draft guidance or informal rules
- [3] An approved policy
- [4] Approved tools and clear data rules
- [5] Approved tools, data rules, spend controls and a way to stop an AI workflow

12. **Has AI changed how a recurring workflow runs?**  
**My answer:** pilots that did not reach day-to-day use. No hesitation.  
**Review:** Best question in the set. It tests operational change rather than
tool adoption. “Production” is rightly avoided. **TWEAK.** Question: “Has AI
changed how any recurring workflow operates day to day, not just how quickly
people do tasks?” Keep the answers.

## Result copy

The stages broadly land. The issue is certainty: a five-minute self-report
cannot confidently say what a company “is”.

- Replace “Your answers and your foundations agree” with: “Your answers are
  consistent with the foundations you described.”
- Replace “N of your 4 areas claim(s) more…” with: “In N of 4 areas, the
  workflow maturity you selected is ahead of the foundations you described.”
- “You’re one workflow away from AI-enabled” oversells. Replace with:
  “You have the beginnings of AI-enabled work. The next practical test is one
  recurring workflow that AI can start and run with clear human decisions.”
- The AI-absent basics offer is too prescriptive. “Markdown” and “projects”
  sound like the consultancy’s preferred method, not my problem. Replace with:
  “In one focused day, we’ll identify where AI can safely help now, agree the
  context and rules it needs, and choose a workflow worth changing.”
- AI-native: replace “Agents run the workflows that run your business” with:
  “AI runs important recurring workflows, with foundations in place to govern
  and improve them.” It is less absolute.

## Page copy: priority issues

1. The methodology section is too long before I start. Replace the scoring
paragraph with: “Your workflow answers set the starting point. The eight
foundation questions can limit how far each answer counts. This keeps a good
pilot from looking like a company-wide operating model.”

2. “Generalised from the agentic maturity model in our team assessment” is
consultancy language and proves nothing. Remove it.

3. Define “agent” once, in plain English: “Here, an agent is AI that can start
and carry out defined steps in a workflow, with people making agreed decisions.”

4. The worked example is useful but overly numerical. Keep it only if shortened
to one paragraph; otherwise it makes a five-minute check-up feel defensive.

5. “Writing the context AI needs in markdown” is product-method language.
Replace with “capturing the business context and instructions AI needs”.

## The three changes that matter most

1. Rewrite questions 6–11, especially systems access, business context and
measurement. They currently reward guesswork or technical fluency.

2. Fix the shared area rungs so private chat use does not blur “not part of the
workflow” with “helps people”.

3. Soften result certainty and sell the working day around my workflow and
operating constraints, not markdown, projects or a promised prototype.
